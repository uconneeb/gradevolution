# **Use of generative artificial intelligence in EEB5449**

The following policy applies to all work that is done for EEB 5449 (based on draft departmental policy)

## **Rationale:**

Our graduate programs are designed to help students learn to think critically. The brain is highly plastic; using it changes it. For this reason, it is essential that students are fully engaged in their graduate work: reading, summarizing, thinking, analyzing, debating, writing, revising. Think of earning an MS or PhD as an athletic feat. An athlete cannot outsource training and then expect to improve their performance.

## **Principles:**

- Uses of generative AI that short-cut the learning process are prohibited.
- You remain accountable for the content of any work you submit for academic credit or to fulfill a requirement.
- You must understand and be able to explain in full the content of any work submitted for credit.
- All uses of AI in completing work for academic credit must be disclosed.

## Uses of AI prohibited by existing policies on plagiarism and academic integrity:

- Submission of AI-generated material (text and figures) as part of any work for credit unless specifically authorized and disclosed.
- Submission of any text that is modified from AI-generated text (e.g., AI writes a draft that you edit).

## **Other examples of prohibited uses of AI:**

- Submitting any writing prompt (or similar question) for a course assignment to AI
- Asking AI to summarize an assigned reading
- Asking AI to compare or contrast assigned readings
- Asking AI to defend a position related to an assignment or to generate a thesis statement for an assignment
- Submitting any text written for a course or degree requirement to AI with a request that AI revise it (whether generally, or with specific prompts, such as a request to make the text more concise, eloquent, or formal, and incorporating those revisions into the final product.
- Submitting feedback you received and asking AI to respond.

What these examples have in common is that they are all ways of using AI to reduce the thinking you are doing.

## **Examples of permissible uses of AI:**

- AI-based checking of grammar and spelling.
- Use of AI-based accessibility tools.
- Asking for vocabulary explanations
- Asking for explanation of a statistical analysis or unfamiliar method.
- Using AI to translate text.
- Asking AI questions about topics you are interested in

What these examples have in common is that they are ways of using AI to learn; they are not different in spirit from looking up a word in a dictionary or reading about an analysis on Wikipedia. Many of these uses are parallel to ways that search engines are used currently. You remain responsible for understanding the answers. As always, caution is recommended in evaluating material provided by AI, as AI is known to make stuff up.

## Disclosure of generative AI use:

If you do use AI, you must include a brief statement describing how you used it and how this influenced your assignment. A sample explanation would be "I asked ChatGPT to define 5 terms I didn't know." or I asked Gemini what the best articles about XXXXXX were. You must also be prepared to share the AI chat logs or similar documentation upon request.

# Appendix: Some Examples

There are gray areas; if you're not sure, discuss it with your advisor, committee or course instructor; what is and is not permissable is context-dependent.

## **Let's start with a non-AI example of context dependence:**

One of my responsibilities as a child was to prepare dinner one night a week during the summer. Would it have been acceptable for me to fulfill this responsibility by ordering pizza?

**Answer**: that depends on what my parents' purpose was. If the purpose had been to give my mom a night off from cooking, then ordering pizza would have been fine. However, since the purpose was to ensure that I gained some basic cooking skills, ordering pizza would have been cheating. If I then decided to make a pizza, using some prepared ingredient (e.g. a jar of roasted red peppers) would be fine; baking a frozen pizza would not be. Academic assignments are generally of the "learn to cook" variety rather than the "get a night off" variety, but they may have components of the "roasted red pepper" type.

## Here's an AI example:

### ChatGPT Conversation:  

Prompt: "What's an appropriate statistical test for phylogenetic signal in a comparative dataset."

Excerpt from ChatGPT's lengthy reply: "For most comparative datasets with continuous traits, **Pagel's λ** and **Blomberg's K** are the two standard complementary measures. K is useful for comparing the strength of signal relative to Brownian motion, while λ is especially valuable because it can be incorporated directly into comparative models such as PGLS."

### Based on this exchange, I prepared the following text:  
"I will test for phylogenetic signal in genome size across the phylogeny using Pagel's λ and Blomberg's K, two standard and complementary measures. Based on Blomberg's K, I will determine whether relatives resemble each other more than expected under a Brownian motion model of evolution. Pagel's λ will be incorporated into phylogenetic least squares (PGLS) models testing for a relationship between genome size and embryonic development time."

**Inclusion of this text in academic work clearly violates the AI use policy. I have plagiarized my response using ChatGPT as my source.**

**Scenario 1:** I fully understand and can explain why the statistical tests are suitable in general terms.

**Remedy:** Explain the plan for statistical analysis in my own words.

**Scenario 2:** I can't explain core ideas, such as phylogenetic signal, Blomberg's K or Pagel's λ, or why phylogenetic structure must be taken into account.

**Remedy:** I must learn more about these concepts in order to be able to explain the plan for statistical analysis in my own words.
