### Description of assignments:

The goal of this class is to give students:

1. the grounding in core concepts of evolutionary biology required to be an effective researcher and educator in EEB,
2. familiarity with foundational and cutting-edge literature, 
3. an introduction to some skills needed to do research and be a member of the academic community. These include the ability to read and critically analyze the literature, identify questions for further study, and to clearly teach complex ideas to others, and
4. to prepare students to succeed in their general exam and later classwork and research.

The assignments are designed to assess student achievement in these areas.

### Reading assignments
In seeking familiarity with the field of evolutionary biology, there is no substitute for extensive, frequent, and deep reading of its literature. There will typically be one assigned reading per class meeting. These will be a mix of foundational classic papers and new papers reflecting the current state of the field. Students are expected to complete these readings in advance of class time. When doing the reading, students should take notes in which they reflect on the paper’s content. On coming to class students should be prepared to explain the paper’s intellectual background, methods, findings, and implications. Students should also flag any weaknesses they see in the paper, and be prepared to bring up at least one point that they did not understand. You may be called upon randomly in class to comment on a paper, and being prepared to respond will contribute towards your participation component of your grade.

### Reading recommendations
The ‘essential’ literature in any field is too large to cover in one semester. To prepare students for their general exam, the EEB faculty recommend that students continue to read additional foundational papers, beyond those assigned in the Evolution core course. To assist this,  the EEB faculty have curated a longer list of key papers (and some books) that they believe represent a core foundation for our EEB students. We strongly recommend reading these before the end of your graduate career. Students are particularly advised to read many of these to prepare for their general exams, if time permits.


## GRADED ASSIGNMENTS

### Weekly reflections
Each Monday the class session will have some combination of discussion, or an activity, and an oral ‘quiz’. To prepare for this, students should submit a weekly reflection via HuskyCT, before the start of class each Friday. This should be a pdf document of up to 2 pages. The weekly reflection should include:

1. One exam question (student’s choice of format) with an answer key, that would be suitable for a graduate level test on Evolution or General Exam. This should evaluate a subtle grasp of key concepts from the week’s material (lecture and readings and related subjects). This is meant to get students thinking about likely questions (and answers) that could comprise the written component of the general exam. A shared library of these questions (and answer keys) will be developed for the whole class.
2. A list of three questions representing things you still do not understand, or do not know, about the topics covered this week.
3. A one paragraph (250 word) mini research proposal based on topics covered in the week’s class. The proposal should clearly:
* Identify a biological question that you might wish to answer about the week’s material.
* Explain why this question is worth answering, in light of the state of the discipline as you understand it.
* In a few sentences, articulate what you might do to obtain an answer to this question. This could describe an observation you would obtain, a model, an experiment, etc.

### Short teach-ins
Each Monday, several students will be selected semi-randomly to answer a question, explain a recent reading, or present a brief explanation about the recent material to the rest of the class. This may entail going slightly beyond the material already covered in class. The prompt for this will be disseminated in advance, and students should come prepared to answer the question in 5 minutes or less, each week. This is meant to give students practice with answering oral questions as a lead up to the oral component of their general exam.

### Problem sets
There will be a small number of problem sets over the duration of the course. These will include activites like conducting simulations or doing simple analysis of some data. Problem set work will generally be done in conjunction with the Monday activities (either as preparation or follow-up). Co-working is encouraged so students can learn from each other; each student must submit their own problem set, and collaboration should be noted.

### Mid-term exam
There will be a written mid-term exam covering the first half of the course material. This will take place in class, and will last 50 minutes. The exam will be composed of short answer questions.

### Final exam
There will be a final exam in the exam period of finals week. This will be a two hour exam, composed of short answer questions.

The final grade for the class will be based on all assignments:

20% | Final Exam
20% | Midterm Exam
10% | Satisfactory completion of problem sets
20% | In-class participation including at least 2 short “teach-ins”
30% | Weekly reflections (at least 10 completed, 3 points each)
